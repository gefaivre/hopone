Every autumn brings a new round of economic-impact estimates for marathons. Three came out in the past week, from very different races.

| Race | Estimated impact | Field | Who produced the figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| St. George Marathon (Utah) | $15 million | 10,000+ runners | City of St. George |
| Techcombank Hanoi International Marathon (2025) | $13.7 million | 13,000+ runners, 55 countries | Sunrise Events Vietnam (organizer) |
| Berlin Marathon (2024) | about €469 million in value creation | 86,000+ athletes over the 2026 race weekend | Race organizer |

## St. George: a race that pays for itself

The **St. George Marathon** runs its 50th edition this year. The first race, in 1976, had 38 finishers; this year’s field passed **10,000** after selling out in three weeks.

The city’s own figures are unusual because they cover costs and revenue as well as visitor spending:

- The city **invests under $500,000** in operating costs.
- It expects to **make about $2 million** this year.
- That revenue goes into youth sports and parks.
- The organizers are aiming for a **$100,000 prize purse** in 2026.

## Hanoi: fast growth from a young race

The **Hanoi International Marathon** contributed **$13.7 million** to the city’s economy in 2025, according to its organizer. The figure comes without a breakdown by spending category. The field has nearly doubled in four editions:

- **7,000 runners** at the first edition in 2022
- **More than 13,000**, from 55 countries and territories, in 2025
- **More than 14,000** expected at the 2026 edition, held October 2–4

## How to read these numbers

These estimates are not comparable with one another:

- **Who produces them differs.** St. George’s figure comes from the city, Hanoi’s and Berlin’s from the organizers.
- **What they measure differs.** Some count direct visitor spending, others broader “value creation.” None of the three articles publishes a full methodology.

They are still useful for something else: they show what organizers put forward when they negotiate with cities over road closures, police and public subsidies. As Dublin Marathon CEO Seán Ó Riain put it this week, “the commercial and economic impact on the city is massive.” That argument carries weight when entry fees go up.
